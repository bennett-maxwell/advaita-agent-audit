# How to Evaluate FDD Item 19: A Buyer's Forensic Guide

Item 19 is the only place in a Franchise Disclosure Document where a brand is allowed to talk about money. It's also the most-misread section of the FDD. Buyers see a headline number, get excited, and miss the fine print that makes the same brand look very different. Here's how to read Item 19 the way a forensic CFO would.

## What Item 19 Is Required to Show

Item 19 is the **Financial Performance Representation (FPR)** section of the FDD. By FTC rule:

- Brands are **not required** to make an Item 19 disclosure at all
- If they do, the disclosure must have a reasonable basis and supporting data
- The disclosure must be in the FDD itself — not in a separate handout, not on a sales call
- Brands may not make earnings claims outside Item 19

If a franchise development rep tells you on a call "we expect you'll make $X," and that number isn't in Item 19, that's an FTC violation. Report it and walk.

## The Six Things to Look For in Item 19

### 1. The denominator
"$1.6M average unit volume" sounds impressive — but average of what? 8 units? 200 units? Year 1 or fully ramped? The denominator changes everything. A 200-unit average with one bad year is statistically honest. An 8-unit average is one outlier away from being marketing.

### 2. Time in operation
Top-quartile second-year units behave nothing like first-year openings. Ask: which cohort is being averaged? Mature units only? All operating units? Brands that show only "open at least 24 months" are giving you a cleaner number but excluding early struggle.

### 3. Top / bottom quartile, not just the average
A brand showing only the mean is hiding the spread. Demand:
- Top quartile (best 25%)
- Median (middle)
- Bottom quartile (worst 25%)

If the bottom quartile is 40% of the top, the brand has variability. That's not necessarily bad — but it tells you executional discipline matters a lot in this system.

### 4. Same-store sales trend
Item 19 sometimes shows revenue. It rarely shows whether existing units are growing or shrinking year over year. Cross-reference with Item 20 (outlet counts) — if total units are flat but the brand is opening new ones, existing units are closing. That's a red flag the headline AUV doesn't surface.

### 5. Cost structure or just revenue
Many Item 19s show revenue only. Revenue without a cost line is a vanity number. The brand may be willing to share unit-level P&L summaries privately with serious buyers under NDA — ask. If they won't, the existing franchisee validation calls (Item 20 contact list) are the next-best source.

### 6. Geographic concentration
A national average that's actually driven by 3 city markets is fragile. Ask whether your specific geography appears in the dataset.

## Six Questions to Ask the Brand on a Discovery Call

1. "What's the denominator on the Item 19 number — which units, which years?"
2. "What does the bottom quartile look like, and what do those units have in common?"
3. "How has same-store performance moved in the last 24 months?"
4. "Is the AUV figure pre-royalty or post-royalty?"
5. "Are the unit-level P&L summaries available under NDA?"
6. "Which of your existing franchisees would you suggest I call to validate the Item 19 number?"

A confident brand will answer all six in writing. A defensive brand will deflect on three or four.

## The Validation Call Pattern That Works

When you call existing franchisees (Item 20 contact list), don't ask "are you happy?" — everyone is socially conditioned to say yes. Ask:

- "What's your unit doing in revenue this trailing twelve months?"
- "What's your owner take-home after all expenses including royalty?"
- "Would you buy a second unit?"
- "What's the one thing the brand doesn't tell new buyers that you wish they did?"

Five calls into this script and a pattern emerges. The pattern is your real Item 19.

## What "Above Average" Actually Means

Brand reps will say "we expect a unit in your market to perform above the system average because [reasons]." This claim is technically an earnings projection and either belongs in Item 19 or is non-compliant. Push back: "If that's true, can you put it in writing as a supplement to the FDD?" The answer tells you whether you're being sold or advised.

## The Bottom Line

Item 19 is not a marketing brochure. It's a regulated disclosure document. Read it the way you'd read an earnings filing for a public company: skeptically, with the cost lines reconstructed, and with calls to corroborating sources. A franchise sale that doesn't survive that kind of review is one you'll regret in year three.
